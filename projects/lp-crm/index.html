LP CRM.

A modular CRM and business operations project exploring how client relationships, tasks, payments, team processes and reporting can work together in one coherent system.

SURAJ KUMARBUILDINGLAST REVIEWED 2026-11-14

Why another CRM concept?

Working across campaigns, tasks, clients and reporting reveals how quickly operational information becomes fragmented. A proposal may sit in email, a client record in a spreadsheet, a task in another tool and an invoice somewhere else. The team may spend more time reconciling what happened than doing the actual work. LP CRM grew from an interest in bringing these workflows together with a clearer sense of ownership and progress.

Existing CRMs and business suites solve many of these problems already, so the project is not based on the idea that every company needs a completely new tool. The opportunity is to explore a system shaped around the workflows of a small consultancy or service team, where project work and client relationships are closely connected.

The modular product blueprint

The design covers client records, projects, tasks, timesheets, leads, proposals, estimates, contracts, invoices, retainers, expenses, payments, staff attendance, leave, notices, internal communication, reports, files and a client portal. A dashboard would connect these modules through a useful operating summary rather than functioning as an isolated collection of charts.

A wide module list is only a product blueprint. The difficult work is deciding which entities are shared, how records move between stages and what each user role can see or change. A task can belong to a project, which belongs to a client, while an invoice may reference several deliverables. Designing these relationships carefully is more important than quickly generating separate screens for every feature.

Architecture and deployment lessons

The implementation experiments use a web application and PostgreSQL-backed state, with Render as a hosting route. Authentication and database configuration have been areas of hands-on testing. A login page working successfully is not enough to declare the platform secure; password updates, session handling, permissions, audit logs, account recovery and data integrity must all be examined.

Every important operation needs a server-side permission check. Hiding an action in the interface does not stop an unauthorised request from reaching the API. Client portals require particular care because multiple organisations may share the application while needing strict separation of their data. The system should be designed so a cross-client data leak is prevented by the backend, not by the user's navigation path.

What makes a CRM genuinely usable

A CRM succeeds when people can use it during the pressure of real work. The interface should make the next important action visible, avoid redundant data entry and preserve a clear history of interactions. A beautiful dashboard has limited value if recording a call requires ten clicks or an invoice cannot be reconciled with a payment.

Good defaults, consistent status labels and simple filtering matter. A user should understand which tasks are due, which proposals await a response, which invoices are unpaid and which enquiries need follow-up. The application must also behave reliably on mobile devices because operational work rarely happens only at a desk.

How I would use AI responsibly

AI can help summarise client conversations, suggest follow-up wording, organise unstructured notes or identify missing fields. It should not silently change contract terms, approve expenses or send private client information to external systems. A model-generated suggestion needs an obvious distinction from verified records, and consequential changes require permissions and review.

The broader goal is an operating system that assists human work rather than obscures it. Reliability, security, auditability and straightforward manual workflows remain the foundation. Automation becomes valuable once those foundations are dependable.

From broad idea to reliable product

The next sensible sequence is to stabilise identity and permission management, choose the most frequently used client and project workflows, verify database operations, and implement reporting that reflects real records. Then expand invoice, time-tracking and client portal functions in manageable slices. Each slice should be tested independently and with data representative of the situations it will encounter.

LP CRM is a project I use to learn about product scope and operating discipline. Its unfinished parts are as important to document as the working screens: the gap between a feature list and a trustworthy product is where much of the engineering work lives.
